# Jobs and internships

Full-time roles, internships, co-ops, apprenticeships, contract work.

## Who reads it

Two readers, in sequence, and they want different things.

**First, a filter.** Often software, often a recruiter spending well under a minute.
At larger companies an applicant tracking system parses your resume into fields before
a human sees it. This reader is matching your document against a requisition, not
evaluating you.

**Then, the hiring manager.** They are asking one question: can this person do the job
on my team, starting soon, without me having to rebuild them. Everything that reads as
potential rather than evidence loses here.

The distinguishing feature: **the resume carries the application, not the essays.**
This inverts the student-org case. Prompts are often optional or absent, and effort
spent on a cover letter is usually better spent making the resume match the posting.

## The artifacts

- Resume, almost always PDF, one page for students and early career
- Sometimes a cover letter, sometimes optional, occasionally the whole application
- A portfolio, GitHub, or work samples for design, engineering, writing, research
- Short application questions: work authorization, location, salary, start date
- Often a take-home, a technical screen, or an online assessment before any human call

## Match the posting, literally

The single highest-leverage move in this type. Read the job posting and pull out the
exact nouns: the tools, the frameworks, the methods, the domain words. If you have
done the thing, use their word for it rather than your synonym. A resume that says
"data pipelines" against a posting that says "ETL" is a worse match for both the
software and the human, for no good reason.

This is not keyword stuffing, and it is not a license to claim anything. It is using
the reader's vocabulary for work you actually did. Run `/resume` after reading the
posting, not before.

## What is usually unasked

| Stated                        | Usually deciding                                                |
| ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Why this company              | Have you read anything about us, or is this application number 60 |
| Tell me about yourself        | Can you give a two-minute answer with a shape to it              |
| A challenge you faced         | Do you know the difference between hard and inconvenient         |
| Why are you leaving           | Are you running toward something or away from something          |
| Where do you see yourself     | Will you leave in eight months                                   |
| Any questions for us          | Did you prepare, or is this a job to you rather than this job    |

The last one is scored more than applicants think, and the failure mode is asking
something the careers page answers.

## Format traps

- A resume that is not a PDF, or a PDF that is an image, which parses as an empty
  document
- Multi-column layouts, tables, and headers or footers, which some parsers mangle
- Dates in a format that does not parse, or missing entirely
- A portfolio link behind a login, or a GitHub with nothing pinned
- Salary and location fields answered inconsistently with the cover letter

## Timeline

Slow and lumpy. Weeks to months, with silence in the middle that is not a signal.
Internship recruiting for large employers often opens the summer before, closes early,
and does not reopen. That lead time is the thing to track in `DEADLINES.md`, since
missing an opening by a month cannot be fixed by a better application.

## The interview

Multiple rounds, each with a different job. Recruiter screen for logistics and
baseline. Technical screen or take-home for capability. Behavioral loop for judgment
and collaboration. Sometimes a final with a senior person for fit and closing.

Behavioral answers want a shape: the situation, what you specifically did, what
happened, in that order, with the middle being the longest part. The most common
failure is spending ninety seconds on context and fifteen on your own contribution.

## References

Usually requested late, sometimes after an offer. Ask people early anyway, since the
lead time is what goes wrong. See `/recommenders`.
