# Graduate school and research programs

Masters and PhD programs, REUs and summer research programs, post-baccs, structured
research placements.

## Who reads it

Faculty, on an admissions committee, reading for **fit with a specific person or lab**
more than for general excellence. At PhD level in most fields, the question underneath
everything is whether a particular professor wants to advise you for five years and
can fund you.

The distinguishing feature: **the reader may become your advisor.** Nowhere else is
the application read by the person who will work with you daily. Generic enthusiasm
for the field reads as someone who has not figured out what they want to work on,
which is the most common reason a strong student gets rejected from a strong program.

## The artifacts

- Statement of purpose, the central document, usually 500 to 1500 words
- Sometimes a separate personal statement or diversity statement, which is a different
  document with a different job, not a rewrite of the first one
- Three letters of recommendation, and at PhD level these are often decisive
- Transcript, sometimes official with lead time
- A writing sample or research sample in many humanities and social science programs
- A CV rather than a resume: longer, with publications, presentations, and coursework

## The statement of purpose is not a personal essay

The most common failure is writing the college-application version: the moment you
fell in love with the subject, told as a story. Programs want a different thing.

Roughly, in order:

1. What you want to work on, specifically enough that it names a problem
2. What you have already done that shows you can do it, in mechanisms and results
3. Why this program and which people, by name, and what about their work connects
4. What you intend to do with the training

The proportions matter. Most of the document is two and three. The origin story, if it
appears at all, is a sentence.

## Naming faculty

Name two or three, not one and not eight. One is a risk if they are not taking
students. Eight reads as a search-and-replace. For each, say something about their
actual work, and make it clear you have read a paper rather than a faculty page.

Check whether they are taking students. This is often on a lab page and it changes
whether the application is worth submitting.

## What is usually unasked

| Stated                          | Usually deciding                                                  |
| ------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Why our program                 | Do you know what we actually do, or did you sort by ranking        |
| Your research interests         | Can you scope a question, or do you only have a field              |
| Your background                 | Can you finish something hard over years without external pressure |
| Your career goals               | Does what you want require this degree                             |
| Diversity statement             | What will you actually do here, beyond having a background         |

## Format traps

- CV formatted as a resume, one page, with the publications cut for space
- A writing sample outside the stated page range, or one that does not show the kind
  of thinking the program cares about
- Letters submitted through a portal on the applicant's deadline, which are really due
  earlier
- Reusing one statement across programs with only the program name changed, which is
  visible immediately in the "why us" paragraph

## Timeline

The longest of any type. Deadlines cluster in December for fall admission, letters
need six to eight weeks of notice, and the work of choosing programs and reading
faculty work takes months before that.

## The interview

Not universal, but common in PhD admissions and increasingly in funded masters
programs. It is a conversation about your research, and the failure mode is being
unable to go one level deeper on your own project. Expect "what would you do next,"
"what was the weakness in that design," and "what did you actually do versus your
advisor."
