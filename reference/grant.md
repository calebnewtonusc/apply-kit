# Grants and funding

Research grants, project funding, nonprofit and foundation grants, community and
microgrants, arts funding, seed and small-business awards.

## Who reads it

A program officer, then reviewers, against **stated funding priorities** and often a
scored rubric that is published or obtainable. Many funders will tell you the rubric
if you ask, and some publish previously funded projects.

The distinguishing feature: **you are not the subject of the application, the project
is.** Every other type is evaluating a person. This one evaluates a plan, with the
person as evidence that the plan will get executed. Applicants who write about
themselves here are answering a question nobody asked.

## The artifacts

- A narrative or project description, tightly structured, often with mandated headings
- A budget, and usually a budget justification explaining each line
- A timeline with milestones
- An evaluation plan: how you will know whether it worked
- Letters of support from partners, which are different from letters of recommendation
- Sometimes organizational documents: tax status, financials, board list

## Structure to their headings, exactly

Funders read many applications against a checklist. If the guidelines name sections,
use those names, in that order, even when your material would flow better another way.
A reviewer looking for your evaluation plan should not have to find it.

## The four things a reviewer is checking

1. **Does this fit what we fund?** Most rejections are here, and most are avoidable by
   reading the priorities before writing. If your project needs to be reframed beyond
   recognition to fit, it is the wrong funder.
2. **Is the plan real?** Specific activities, with dates, with named people
   responsible. Vagueness reads as a project that has not been thought through.
3. **Can these people execute it?** Track record, relevant capacity, partners already
   committed rather than hoped for.
4. **Will we be able to tell whether it worked?** Baseline, measure, and what number
   would count as success. Say the number.

## What is usually unasked

| Stated                        | Usually deciding                                                  |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Describe the need             | Do you know this community, or did you read about it              |
| Your project plan             | Has this been thought through past the idea                      |
| Your organizational capacity  | If we fund this, will it actually happen                          |
| Sustainability                | What happens to this the day our money runs out                  |
| Budget                        | Do these numbers correspond to the activities you just described  |

The budget is read as a truth check on the narrative. A narrative promising six
workshops and a budget with materials for two is the single fastest way to lose
credibility.

## Format traps

- Page and character limits that include references and appendices
- Required attachments that must be separate files with specific names
- Indirect cost rules, matching requirements, and eligible expense categories that
  quietly disqualify a budget
- Portals with registration steps that take days, which cannot be done the night before

## Timeline

Registration and eligibility steps first, which have their own lead time. Then
letters of support, which depend on partners. Deadlines are usually hard and portals
usually close on the minute.

## What counts as a specific

A named partner who has agreed. A number with a baseline. A previously funded project
of theirs that yours extends or complements. The exact population, with a count.

## After a rejection

Many funders will give feedback on request, and many fund a resubmission. Ask. This is
the type where reapplying is most normal and most successful, and where `/reapply` is
worth running with the actual reviewer comments in hand.
