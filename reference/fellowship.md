# Fellowships, scholarships, and awards

Named fellowships, merit scholarships, research awards, leadership programs, national
competitions, funded summer programs.

## Who reads it

A committee, not one person. Usually three to seven readers, often faculty, alumni, or
program staff, each scoring independently against **published criteria**. Then they
meet and argue about the borderline cases.

Two things follow from that, and they change how you write.

**The criteria are public and they are the rubric.** Almost every fellowship publishes
what it selects for. That document is worth more than any general advice about essay
writing, and most applicants skim it once and never map their answers to it. Map every
essay to a named criterion before drafting.

**You need a champion in the room.** Committee decisions get made by one reader
arguing for you. Give them the sentence they will use. That sentence is almost always
a specific, unusual, verifiable thing, not a summary of your qualities.

## The artifacts

- Two to five essays, longer than any other type, often 500 to 1000 words each
- A personal statement that overlaps with but is not identical to the essays
- Letters of recommendation, usually two to four, often the deciding factor
- A transcript, sometimes an official one with lead time
- Sometimes a proposed project, a budget, or a plan for the funded period
- Sometimes an interview round, often panel format

## What is usually unasked

| Stated                          | Usually deciding                                                 |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Describe your leadership        | Did you lead something, or did you hold a title                    |
| Your goals for the fellowship   | Do you need this specific thing, or any prize with money attached  |
| A challenge you overcame        | Do you know the difference between hard and inconvenient           |
| Your commitment to service      | Is there a pattern here, or one thing you did before applying      |
| Why you                         | What happens to this money if we give it to the next person instead|
| Your long-term plan             | Is the plan real enough that we can picture year three             |

The money question is the one applicants miss. A committee is allocating a scarce
thing. The strongest answers make the case that this particular fellowship changes
what happens next, in a way another one would not.

## Format traps

- Character limits rather than word limits, counted with spaces
- Portal systems that time out, lose drafts, or strip formatting
- Recommenders who must submit through the portal by the same deadline, which means
  their deadline is really two weeks earlier than yours
- A separate institutional endorsement or campus nomination step with its own earlier
  deadline, which disqualifies people who find out late

## Timeline

Long. Two to six months from opening to decision, with recommender lead time as the
binding constraint. Campus nomination deadlines for the big national fellowships can
run six months before the national deadline.

Everything here goes in `DEADLINES.md` under things with lead time, and the
recommender dates go in first.

## What counts as a specific

A named criterion from their own published rubric, matched to a named thing you did.
An alum of the fellowship and what they did with it. The specific constraint the money
removes: not "it would help me focus on research" but "it replaces the twenty hours a
week I currently work in dining services."

## The interview

Panel, often with people outside your field. They will ask you to explain your work to
someone who does not share your vocabulary, and that is a scored test rather than a
courtesy. They will also press on the plan: what happens if the project fails, what
you would do without the funding.
