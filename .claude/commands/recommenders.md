description: Pick recommenders, ask them well, and give them what they need to write a strong letter
---

Load `application-types`. Recommenders are central for `fellowship` and `grad-school`,
late but real for `job`, a different artifact for `grant` (letters of support commit an
organization, not a person), and rare for `student-org`.

Write `you/RECOMMENDERS.md` and add every date to `DEADLINES.md`.

## The thing that goes wrong

Not letter quality. **Lead time.** A letter due through a portal on the applicant's
deadline is really due from the recommender a week earlier, and the ask should go out
weeks before that. Every year people lose applications they had already written,
because the letter did not land.

Say the real dates in the first line of the file:

| Org | Applicant deadline | Recommender's real deadline | Ask sent by |

Six to eight weeks of notice for grad school and major fellowships. Three weeks is a
tight minimum for anything. Under a week, the honest advice is to ask someone who
knows the applicant well enough to write fast, and to say plainly that it is short
notice.

## Picking them

The best letter comes from **specificity, not seniority**. A postdoc who supervised the
applicant daily writes a better letter than a department chair who knows their name. A
famous person's generic letter is read as exactly what it is.

Rank candidates against three questions:

1. Can they describe something the applicant did, with detail, from having watched it?
2. Do they have a basis to compare: "top three of the forty students I have advised"?
3. Will they actually submit on time? This is a real criterion and people know the
   answer.

Cover different dimensions rather than three versions of the same relationship.
Academic, work, and something the applicant built or led is a stronger set than three
professors from the same department.

If a program specifies a type of recommender, that requirement outranks all of this.

## The ask

Draft it. Short, direct, and easy to decline:

- What it is and the real deadline, stated as a date
- The one thing you hope they can speak to, named specifically, which is the part that
  makes a letter concrete
- An explicit out: "if you do not think you could write a strong letter, I would much
  rather know now"
- What you will send if they say yes

That out is not politeness. A lukewarm letter from someone who felt cornered does more
damage than a missing one, and giving them the door is how you avoid it.

## The packet

Once they say yes, send one message with everything, and never make them ask for it:

- Deadline and exactly how to submit, with the portal link
- Resume or CV
- The statement or essays, even in draft
- **A short note on what this application is for and which criteria it scores against**,
  from the org's file
- Two or three specific things the applicant did with them, with dates and details.
  Not a draft of the letter, and not a list of adjectives. Reminders of moments.
- Anything the program explicitly wants the letter to address

Recommenders are writing several of these. The packet is the difference between a
letter about the applicant and a letter about a student they had once.

## Then track it

A row per letter with status, and a reminder date one week before the recommender's
real deadline. Drafting the reminder is part of this command: one line, friendly, no
apology.

Say plainly which letters are at risk and which are done. Anything not confirmed
submitted is not done, and portals do not always notify the applicant.

Afterward, write the thank-you and record the outcome for them in `/outcome`. People
who write letters want to know what happened, and they will be asked again next cycle.
