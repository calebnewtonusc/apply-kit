Two modes. Ask which one, in one line, then go.

**Before**: prep for an info session, a coffee chat, or a message to a member of $1.
**After**: capture what they learned into `you/CHATS.md` and into that org's
`applications/` file.

Talking to one member is the highest-yield thirty minutes in the whole cycle. It
produces the tier-one specific that `/whyus` cannot find online, it tells you what the
org actually worries about, and at many orgs the person you talked to is in the room
when your application comes up.

## Before mode

Read the org's `applications/` file, `you/STORY-BANK.md`, and `TARGETS.md`. Then
write, in under 300 words:

**Five questions worth asking.** Not questions whose answers are on the website. The
ones that work:

- What does a project actually look like week to week?
- What surprised you about it after you joined?
- Who does well here and who bounces off?
- What is the part people underestimate?
- What did your application say that you think got you in?

**One thing to mention about yourself**, chosen for this org, in one sentence. Not a
pitch. Something that gives them a reason to remember which applicant you were.

**What not to do.** Do not ask them to look at your application. Do not ask what the
admit rate is. Do not take notes so visibly that it stops being a conversation.

If it is a cold message rather than a meeting, draft it: three sentences, one specific
reason it is them, one clear ask, no paragraph about their impressive background.

## After mode

Ask what they said, then write it down while it is fresh. Into `you/CHATS.md`:

- Who, their role, the date, and how it happened.
- **Exact phrases they used** about the org. This is the most valuable part. An org's
  own words about itself, from a member rather than a marketing page, are what makes a
  "why us" sentence sound like you were there.
- What they said the org actually worries about in applicants.
- Anything factual: project names, the cycle, the time commitment, whether the
  published deadline is real.
- Whether they offered anything, and what you owe them.

Then update the org's `applications/` file: put the usable specifics under
**Specifics**, correct any fact the research got wrong, and rewrite the **angle** if
what they said changes it. It often does.

Say plainly which single sentence from that conversation should end up in the draft,
and whether naming the person is appropriate for this org. At most orgs it is, and
it is the strongest thing a "why us" answer can contain. At a few it reads as
name-dropping, and the fix is to use what they said without saying who said it.

## Both modes

Anything they told you goes in as a quote or a fact, never as a claim about the org
you did not check. If a member says something that contradicts the published
application, the member is usually right about culture and the form is right about
deadlines.
