# The interview round

Plenty of people write their way into interviews and then lose there. If that is the
pattern, the written material is not the problem and more drafting will not fix it.

## The frame that matters

**The application is the interview's question bank.** Consulting and startup clubs
interview off the page in front of them, line by line, and the follow-up is always
"walk me through that."

So the first prep step is not practice questions. It is going down the resume and the
drafts and asking, for every number and claim: could you answer "how did you get
that?" in ninety seconds. Anything that fails goes on the drill list or comes off the
page.

## Five stories, prepared cold

Not scripts. Five real situations, each held in memory with enough detail that they
can be told from any angle. Choose them to cover different dimensions:

1. **Something you shipped**, including what went wrong on the way
2. **Something you handed off or lost**, told without bitterness
3. **A collapse you handled**, where the situation was genuinely bad
4. **A person you helped**, small scale, no heroics
5. **A time you were wrong**, with a real cost

Almost any behavioral question maps onto one of these. Write them in
`you/STORY-BANK.md` with the specifics: names, numbers, what happened at the worst
moment, what happened after.

Then map them to the club's published rubric if one exists. Many service orgs publish
exactly what they score.

## Structure that buys thinking time

In a consulting-style interview, asking clarifying questions and saying "let me take
thirty seconds to structure this" is **expected and scored.** The move that buys
processing time is the same move that earns points.

Make it the default rhythm rather than something reached for when stuck. Used every
time, it reads as method. Used once under pressure, it reads as hesitation.

## Running a mock

- Ask one question. Wait for the full answer. Do not coach mid-answer.
- **Always ask the follow-up.** The follow-up is where interviews are actually lost.
  "What would you have done differently?" "Who disagreed with you?" "How did you get
  that number?" "What happened to it after you left?"
- Ask the question they are dreading. Once per session.
- Time the answers. Over two minutes on a behavioral question is a problem worth
  naming.
- After each, give three lines: what landed, what a real interviewer would push on,
  and one specific change. Then move on. Do not give a lecture.

## Hard questions worth rehearsing out loud

Anything sensitive in their history: a gap, a leave, a transfer, a bad semester, a
reversal they made publicly. **Rehearse the sentence out loud until it is boring.**
Unrehearsed, it comes out either apologetic or as five minutes of explanation, and
both are worse than the fact itself.

The target is one or two flat sentences, then a return to the present. What they
disclose and what they do not is their call, and it lives in `you/DISCLOSURE.md`.
Honor it without relitigating.

## The question everyone gets and nobody prepares

"You are applying to several clubs. If they all said yes, which would you pick?"

If they have applied to multiple clubs in one lane, especially ones with conflicting
mandatory meetings, they will be asked. Have the answer ready and have it be honest.

## Diagnosing a past failure

If they have lost interviews before, ask what actually happened in the room: ran long,
froze, got a case they had not seen, talked past the question, could not recall a
number. Each has a different drill. Prepping against a guess wastes their week.
