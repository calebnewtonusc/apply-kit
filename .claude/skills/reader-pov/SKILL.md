# Reading as the reader

This is not a rules check. A rules check tells you the answer is 12 words over and
contains a banned phrase. This tells you what a person will notice, feel, and decide.

Run it on the **whole packet at once**, not one answer at a time. Most of what this
catches only exists across answers.

## The three people

Read the packet three times, once as each.

**The first-round screener.** A sophomore, 11pm, 200 left in the pile, scoring on a
form with four boxes. Sixty seconds. They are looking for a reason to stop reading.

Report: which answer would make them stop, where their attention drops, whether the
first sentence of each answer earned the second, and whether anything made them
uneasy in a way they could not articulate.

**The specialist doing diligence.** The club officer who actually knows the domain and
will interview you if you pass. They read for whether the claims hold.

Report: every number they would ask "how did you get that," every claim that reads
bigger than it probably is, every place where the same fact appears differently in two
answers or between an answer and the resume.

**The future teammate.** Someone deciding whether they want to be assigned to a
project with you for fifteen weeks.

Report: do you sound like a person or a candidate, is there one moment of actual
warmth or humor, does anything read as someone who cannot be told they are wrong, and
would they be glad or tired to see your name on their team.

## What to look for that a rules check misses

**The same shape four times.** Read every "why us" answer in a row. If they all run
"here is what I built, here is what I lack, teach me," the reader notices the pattern
even if each one is good. Vary at least one.

**Weight with nowhere to land.** A hard subject raised inside a word limit too small to
resolve it. The reader gets the weight and none of the resolution, and closes the tab
uneasy. This is a container problem, not a subject problem: same story, bigger
question, works fine.

**The unexplained hook.** "After the year I just had." Points at something the reader
does not know, and a stranger reads it as a warning rather than as depth. Say the
thing or drop the reference.

**Tonal whiplash.** A joke immediately after something heavy. The fun questions come
after the serious ones on most forms, and the reader's shoulders have just come down.
Notice where the room changes.

**Density of one theme.** If one subject appears in five of ten answers, that is a
texture observation, not automatically a problem. Reusing your strongest material
across different clubs is what every applicant does and what clubs expect. Say what
you noticed, give a read, and let the applicant decide. Do not argue that a reader
going through 200 applications is cross-referencing one person, because they are not.

**Answers that are too short.** Anything under 60 percent of the limit next to answers
at 95 percent reads as declining interest by the end of the form.

## How to write the report

Numbered findings, most consequential first. For each:

1. What the reader notices, in their words.
2. Which answer, quoted.
3. Whether you would change it, and your actual recommendation. Not "consider whether."

End with the one change that would most improve the packet, and be specific about
which answer and what to do.

## When they push back

They know their life. If they say a finding is wrong, they are usually right, and the
right response is to withdraw the argument in writing rather than restate it softer.
If a piece of the finding survives for a different reason, keep that piece and say
plainly which part you are withdrawing and why.
