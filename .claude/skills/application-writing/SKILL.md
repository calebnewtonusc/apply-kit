# Writing club applications

## Who reads this

One person, on a couch, at 11pm, with somewhere between 80 and 400 applications
left. They are scoring against a rubric that lives mostly in their head. They spend
60 to 90 seconds on yours unless something makes them slow down.

They are not looking for the best applicant. They are looking for a reason to move
you to the yes pile so they can stop thinking about you. Give them one in the first
sentence.

By the time they reach you they have read the same essay forty times: passionate
about the intersection of business and technology, drawn to your commitment to
excellence, eager to learn from a community of driven individuals. Every one of those
essays was written by a real person with a real life. The essays just did not contain
any of it.

## Openings

The first sentence decides whether the rest gets read or skimmed. Three that work:

**A concrete scene, mid-action.** "Forty-eight hours before the event, all eleven
volunteers backed out." No setup, no context. The reader supplies it.

**A number that raises a question.** "I have changed my intended major four times and
I can defend every one of them."

**A claim they could disagree with.** "The attention economy is not a shallowness
machine. It is a self-focus machine." Contestable claims are interesting. Mission
statements are not. A reader who mildly disagrees is a reader who is still reading.

Three that kill it:

- Restating the question. "There are many reasons I want to join X."
- "I have always been passionate about." They have read this forty times tonight.
- A quote or dictionary opener. Even a good quote costs you your only free sentence.

## Specificity is the whole game

Every applicant claims initiative, leadership, and curiosity. Nobody can claim your
details.

| Generic                    | Specific                                                                        |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| I led a large organization | I ran a 90-person club and a coalition of 20 others                             |
| We improved the process    | Cut check-in from 25 minutes to 6 by pre-printing badges                        |
| Interested in consulting   | I want to know why the nonprofit I worked at ran three overlapping intake forms |
| Passionate about design    | I redrew our flyer six times because nobody could find the date                 |

Names, numbers, mechanisms. If you cannot put a number on it, put a mechanism on it:
what specifically you did that changed what specifically happened.

**Never invent the number.** If it is not known, write `[NEED: how many]` and ask.

## Answer the question that was asked

The most common failure is not bad writing. It is answering an adjacent, easier
question.

- "A time you failed" is not "a time I struggled and then triumphed." Give a real
  failure with a real cost you did not fully recover. That is the test: can you look
  at yourself honestly.
- "Why this club" is not "why this field." If the answer works for three clubs, it is
  not an answer to this one.
- "A time you disagreed with a teammate" is not "a time I was right." Resolution
  matters more than vindication.
- "Tell us about yourself" is a trap for people who list. Pick one thread and follow
  it all the way down.

## The two questions under almost every prompt

Applications ask ten things and score two. See the `unasked-questions` skill for the
full method, but these two run under nearly every prompt:

**Why us and not the six others like you?** If you are applying to several clubs in
one lane, every one of them knows it. Any sentence in a "why us" answer that would
work for a different club is a wasted sentence. Name one specific thing: a project
they ran, an alum outcome, a workshop you attended, a person you talked to. Never
their website adjectives back at them.

**Are you here to learn or to be impressive?** This one cuts hardest against strong
applicants. People who have actually shipped things fail it constantly by arriving as
someone who already knows. Naming your gap honestly is the move that works: "the work
felt like consulting, but I was doing it on intuition." That gives the club a job.

## The "why us" trap

Write five applications without watching for this and all five have the same shape:
here is what I built, here is what I cannot do yet, please teach me.

Vary it. Some answers should be **what you will do for them**, in specific terms. "You
run four client projects a semester and none of them have a person who can build the
thing at the end. I can build the thing." That is a different sentence than anything
else in the pile.

## Word limits are a design constraint, not a trim job

The limit determines which story you can tell, before you write a word.

A 75-word answer cannot hold a story that needs setup. If you open a hard subject with
no room to land it, the reader gets the weight and none of the resolution, and they
close the tab uneasy. That is worse than a smaller story told completely.

So pick the story that fits, then use 90 to 100 percent of the limit. An answer at 40
percent reads as low effort even when it is tight, because the reader is comparing you
to someone who used all 250.

## The fun questions are real questions

Favorite snack. A song that describes your life. Your life as a TV show. These are not
filler. This is the club deciding whether they want to sit next to you on Tuesday
nights for a semester.

Answer with an actual specific thing you actually like. Strategic answers are visible
from space and read as someone who cannot relax. Specific and slightly odd beats
clever every time.

One caution: an answer that depends on something painful the reader does not know
about you will land differently than you expect. If it needs backstory they do not
have, pick a different answer.

## Repetition

**Within one application:** never tell the same story twice. The reader sees the whole
form and it makes you look like you have one experience.

**Across different clubs:** reuse your strongest material freely. A reader going
through 200 applications is not cross-referencing one applicant, and every applicant
reuses. Just re-angle it toward what each club actually asked.

## Structure inside an answer

For anything over 100 words:

1. Concrete opening, no setup
2. What you did, in mechanisms
3. What happened, with a number if a real one exists
4. What it changed about how you think

Cut the fourth if you are over the limit. It is the part everyone writes and the part
that reads most like everyone.

## Numbers have to match across every document

The same claim appears on the resume, in three essays, and in the interview. If it
grows between documents, that is the thing that gets caught, and it usually gets
caught in the room rather than on the page.

Pick the version you can defend, write it in `you/PROFILE.md`, and use that one
everywhere. A smaller number you can walk someone through beats a bigger one you
cannot.

## Before calling an answer done

- Could a stranger swap this into their form unchanged? If yes, not finished.
- Is every claim true, yours, and consistent with every other document?
- Are you at 90 to 100 percent of the limit?
- Does the first sentence earn the second?
- Did you answer the question they asked, or the one you wanted?
- Go down its unasked-questions list. Could a reader with only your answer in front of
  them answer each one? Three misses is your revision.
